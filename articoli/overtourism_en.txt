title: Overtourism between TikTok and Classism
publication: 2025-02-16
text: 
Talks of overtourism are increasing, referring to cities filled with tourists where the quality of life for locals consistently worsens.

Various forms of protest occur: locals from Barcelona using water guns against unsuspecting vacationing families, threatening stickers on lockers for keys, street demonstrations. The initial responses: limiting Airbnb in some cities (like in Paris), removing lockers in Rome, introducing entrance tickets to Venice.

However, as always, protesting does not exempt us from understanding; where all this comes from; what realistic alternatives are there. 
A useful example: The Huns of Roccaraso began their invasion after seeing a video of a Neapolitan TikToker. Immediate choruses of disgust. They litter, pollute and don't spend. Here three things are important: the Huns, TikTok and spending.
The first and last ones are the same. If you'd like, you can trace it back to the grand tour, the beginning of the shift from traveling to tourism. The wealthy cultured English would go discover the beauties of Europe. Moving ahead to the second half of the 20th century, beach vacationing and mass tourism were born.
Travel primarily remained domestic, as foreign travels were complicated and expensive. There were no low-cost companies, passports were required, and you were almost obliged to stay in hotels. Yet, movement began. A lot was spent and relative few traveled.

Then came Easyjet, Rynair, Airbnb, and everything exploded. The Huns arrived. Those contemptible beings who don't earn enough to afford hotels and stay in furnished flats from Mondo Convenienza (if you're lucky, Ikea). Revenue for cities increased, private income increased. Everyone participated in the game: low-cost companies got paid by airports, airports by cities, property prices rose, restaurants boomed. There's a catch: more money but the average spending per tourist dramatically decreases. At a certain point, the system starts to clog. More people means more discomfort, more infrastructure to maintain, more congested streets, public spaces exclusively dedicated to visitors. And if the added people bring proportionally less money but the same discomforts, they become increasingly disliked. Roccaraso is the perfect case. Buses are paid from Naples, at most a hot chocolate is consumed, they don't stay there; and the majority of people don't ski thus won't buy a ski pass. The Huns of Roccaraso become Huns, i.e. barbarians to be driven back, perhaps armed with water guns arriving from Barcelona. Behind the apparent motivations (logistical issues, environmental problems, etc.) there's a single true one: they don't spend. That is the problem, the gravest one. The tourist is welcome as long as they spend. We are back at square one. 

In the meantime, TikTok. Thousands of people just for a video. At Cinque Terre this summer, an American approached me and asked 'but where is the photo spot?'. I was supposed to know which photo, it was obvious. I remember well he didn't ask about photos or a famous photo. He was thinking of a specific one. Riomaggiore was that photo. We were at a somewhat isolated spot - yes there are also such places there - with only local youngsters, the sun was high and the sea gorgeous. But it wasn't enough, the important thing is to find the photo spot, i.e. reconnecting with the idea that brought us there, not connecting with the place.
Different and distant places never existed as such but have always been mediated by narration - remember the grand tour: it was nothing more than intellectuals who had an imagination guided by the Greek and Latin classics they had read. However, now this narration is pervasive, monopolizing, and explicit. Which means, starting from the bottom: locations are presented to us with images or reels, therefore perfectly clear, without the nuances that books, legends or friends' stories left. These images, even when proposed from different sources, are few. We always have the same waterfall taken from the same angle, the same dive, the same carbonara dish with pistachio grains. You could almost imagine an app for your phone that takes automatic photos once the GPS recognizes you've arrived at the Instagrammable point (we will explore another time how places are transformed to facilitate this aspect). Finally, the narration is pervasive, hammering us into constantly thinking about the same places in the same way. TikTok and Instagram are the vehicles of all contemporary fantasies (there's no pornography but there are endless semi-naked bodies, so that's sufficient). And social media - much more so today than in the past - don't live from a thousand interactions and a thousand posts, but from a million interactions under one viral photo. Therefore, places to visit aren't a thousand, but one. And that place will be flooded with tourists. Thus, overtourism is a perfectly natural reaction to our way of directing desire. Few places, constantly proposed in a univocal and reductionist manner. I have read somewhere that 90% of tourists visit 10% of the 'attractions' (a word that could already be discussed). And you have immediate proof if you follow the chain of people snaking from the station to St. Mark's in Venice. If you dare, foolishly, to break off and take a side street, you will find yourself alone in a few meters.

We have the perfect mix: we are bombarded with content that always shows us the same few wonderful spots that we want to reach just to take a photo, traveling is increasingly cheap, and there are billions of people ready to afford it. Because we are only at the beginning, 300 million Americans are nothing compared to 3 billion Chinese and Indians coming out of poverty.
Understanding this: let's abolish Airbnb, let's send the TikToking Huns back to their suburban homes, and let's stay in our hotels with our trusty Routard guide. Finally. But who gives us the right to do that? After the rich Westerners have visited the world, are we prepared to close it off to the poor Westerners, and to the non-Westerners who are not extremely wealthy? Do we do like we did with the environment, polluting to the right and center for two centuries and then pontificating in the last twenty years about the Indians who fill the ocean with plastic?
The temptation is there because it's easier to find a pleasing aesthetic solution rather than saying that there may not be an ethical solution that holds up. Everybody will want to visit Rome and Venice or that new spot they've seen online. Let's prevent them from arriving by removing Airbnb. Let's prevent them from desiring by removing their social media. Or let's create mass education centers to force everyone to promote conscious tourism. Do what you can. But the terms of the game are these and there are no solutions that don't smack of an incurable snobbism.
